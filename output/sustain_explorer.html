SuStaIn, visually

Journal-club companion for Taylor et al. (2024). Part 1 is a toy you can poke. Part 2 shows real output from the official pySuStaIn package on simulated data, where we change one thing, the noise, and watch what breaks.

Part 1 · the idea

Every dot is one patient, scanned once

Imagine 3 brain regions (temporal, insula, frontal) that each shrink as the disease advances, measured in z-scores (0 = healthy, 3 = very abnormal). The disease has two possible routes: Subtype A hits the temporal lobe first, Subtype B hits the frontal lobe first. Nobody follows one patient for 15 years, so all we get is a cloud of snapshots. SuStaIn's job is to see the two routes inside the cloud.

Subtype A route (temporal first) Subtype B route (frontal first) Lines = the noise-free route, stage 0 → 7. Click a dot to inspect that patient →

Selected patient

Each cell = "how well does this patient match subtype s at stage k?" (normalised so everything sums to 100%). Hover a cell to see what that hypothesis predicts.

Try this. (1) Set colours to Nothing and lower the noise: you can see two arms. Raise the noise and they melt together. (2) Click a dot near the centre (early stage): the heat map has no clear winner, because early on both routes look like "everything normal". (3) Click a dot far along an arm: one row lights up. That is the whole algorithm in miniature, stage = how far along, subtype = which arm.

What is simplified here: the real algorithm doesn't know the routes. It learns them from the cloud (alternating between "assign patients" and "update the routes", plus MCMC for uncertainty). In this toy I hand it the true routes so you can see only the scoring step. Part 2 is the real learning.

Part 2 · real pySuStaIn output

Same patients, more noise: what does SuStaIn recover?

This is the official tutorial's simulated z-score dataset (5 biomarkers × 3 severity levels = 15 events, 2 true subtypes, 500 people of whom 100 are controls). I kept the random seed fixed, so the ground-truth routes and every patient's true subtype/stage are identical at every noise level. The only thing that changes is the measurement noise. Noise 1.0 is exactly the tutorial's default. "Noise 2.0" means the same biology with half the signal-to-noise ratio: the event levels sit at z = 0.5, 1, 1.5 instead of 1, 2, 3, so they hide inside the scatter of healthy people. (I tell the model the true levels, so it isn't confused by anything except noise.)

Part 3 · for the 3-minute slot

  1. Snapshots, not a movie. Each person is scanned once. SuStaIn lines the snapshots up into routes (subtypes) and positions along a route (stages).
  2. It learns the order of events, not the timing. Stage 10 means "10 abnormal-events have happened on this route", not "10 years in".
  3. My experiment: same patients, signal-to-noise from 2× better to 3× worse. Per-patient subtype accuracy slid steadily (96% → 71.5%) and stage error quadrupled (0.7 → 2.9 of 15), but the order of events stayed almost perfect up to noise 1.5 (Spearman ≥ 0.97) and was still 0.8–0.9 at noise 3. The population-level story is more robust than any single patient's label.
  4. Where it fails: subtype is least reliable for the earliest and latest stages, because early on both routes look "all normal" and at the end both look "all abnormal". The paper's S2/S3/S4 fuzziness may partly be this.
  5. What I took away: Researchers found four different patterns of brain shrinkage in a language-related dementia. Only one pattern clearly matched a diagnosis, and the others overlapped, so the current diagnosis labels don't tell the whole story.
  6. Question for the lab: What would you want to see in a follow-up study to know whether these four subtypes are real, and not just a product of the algorithm?
